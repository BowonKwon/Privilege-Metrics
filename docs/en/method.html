Method and limits

What was counted

English invention privileges and patents come from two lists: Hulme (1896, 1900), who drew 55 industrial monopoly grants of 1561–1603 from the calendars of the Patent Rolls, and Woodcroft (1854), whose chronological list numbers 1 to 14,359 for 1617–1852. No list covers 1604–1616; Hulme himself notes that the return ordered by the Commons in 1827 was never presented.

The Stationers' Register is read straight from the Stationers' Register Online TEI (1557–1640, 10,453 entries). Venetian printing privileges are Fulin's 256 documents (1882), read and coded one by one. The Dutch figures are a recount of the data published by Buning and Looijesteijn.

Venetian invention patents were not counted here. The only record-level source is Berveglieri's printed calendar (2020); figures from the literature are marked 'secondary' and kept apart.

Reading Woodcroft's list

The word coordinates of the Internet Archive OCR were used to separate the margin column (surnames, sealing dates) from the text column (numbers and abstracts). Because numbers must run from 1 to 14,359, the longest rising chain of number-like lines was kept and candidates caught in its gaps were renumbered in order. 115 pages on which the OCR lost entry numbers were read again from the page images with tesseract 5. 36 numbers still have no text: they are counted in the yearly totals (with their neighbours' year) and left out of metrics that need the text.

Dates are the sealing dates printed in the margin. For 13,615 entries the margin date is used as read. Where it could not be read, or its year differed by more than two from the median year of the 20 nearest dated entries, the neighbours' year was used when both neighbours agreed (717 entries).

Terms, rents, provisos, foreign links and shared grants are judged from the words of the abstract. From about 1820 the abstracts stop stating the term, so later terms are left blank; the statutory 14 years was not assumed.

Field classification

There are 17 fields. Word rules are applied to the title of the invention in a fixed order and the first match is taken. Entries before 1700 do not set the title off in quotation marks, so the whole abstract is used, after early-modern spellings are normalised.

The rules were fixed before any results were seen but were revised three times before publication. r2 removed the bare word 'instrument' (any device, in 17th-century usage) from the instruments field; r3 added the spelling table and widened the text used for entries before 1700. These two followed an implausible first result.

Checked against an independent coder's recoding of 212 entries from the text (R2), the r3 rules agreed on 144/212 (68%, 95% CI 61–74%) and on only 2 of 11 mining entries: 'tan pits', 'pit coal' and 'smelting copper ore' had gone to mining and gas lighting to chemicals. After the r4 fix, precision was measured again on a fresh sample of 212 entries drawn with another seed (R2b), not on the sample that prompted the fix. The field figures on the reliability cards are from R2b. The revisions are recorded at the head of src/fields.py.

Reliability cards

Image check (R1): an independent coder read 120 entries, 30 per period, from the page images (number, date, number of grantees, 'communication', term) for comparison with the extracted values. Year agreement 119/120 (99%, 95% CI 95–100%), full date 112/120 (93%, 95% CI 87–97%), term 114/120 (95%, 95% CI 90–98%), number of grantees 112/120 (93%, 95% CI 87–97%), communication 119/120 (99%, 95% CI 95–100%).

Text recoding (R2b): another coder coded 212 entries (11 per field, 25 'other') from the text alone: field, address abroad, communication, term. Field agreement 145/212 (68%, 95% CI 62–74%) (κ 0.67; the sample takes equal numbers per field, so per-field precision weighted by actual field shares gives 67%), address abroad 205/212 (97%, 95% CI 93–98%), communication 208/212 (98%, 95% CI 95–99%), term 210/212 (99%, 95% CI 97–100%).

Fulin recoding (R3): a second coder recoded 40 random documents under the same codebook. Agreement on privilege or not 40/40 (100%, 95% CI 91–100%), term 40/40 (100%, 95% CI 91–100%), beneficiary type 37/40 (92%, 95% CI 80–97%).

Hulme's 55 grants: another coder read the page images independently; dates agree 55/55 (100%, 95% CI 93–100%) and terms 55/55 (100%, 95% CI 93–100%). The decade counts match Hulme's own table on p. 52 (23, 12, 14, 6, 0).

Checks against prior figures

Woodcroft's numbered entries come to 2,226 for 1660–1799 against MacLeod's (1988) 2,240 (a difference of 14), and 13,052 for 1700–1850 against the 13,070 of Nuvolari et al. (2021) (a difference of 18). The counting rules of those studies (whether extensions are included, for instance) were not checked, so the source of the differences is not settled.

The decade totals of the Stationers' Register match the SRO website's own search counts in every decade. The Fulin count of 240 privileges for 1492–1526 is 14 below Kostylo's (2010) 254, possibly because of what each counts as a privilege.

What this site does not do

It makes no causal estimates. Years such as 1474, 1624 and 1710 are reference points for before-and-after comparison only; the site does not say that a law increased patenting.

Record-level counts of Venetian invention patents, French royal privileges, the Stationers' Register after 1640 and United States federal patents (from 1790) are candidates for later versions.
